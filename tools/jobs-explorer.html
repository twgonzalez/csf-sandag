Check Your City's Jobs Number

Jobs helped decide the last housing allocation, and several cities doubt the jobs credited to them. This page shows, for each of the 19 jurisdictions, where an open jobs count comes from — the census-visible jobs, the military layer, the headquarters corrections — next to the 2019 figures, down to the census block. Every number is precomputed by a public pipeline; this page only displays it.

An independent analysis, built entirely on public data so every claim can be checked. It offers no recommendations — it exists to raise better questions before the seventh cycle's decisions arrive. Think a number is wrong? Say so — that is what open methodology is for.

How this count is built — and how to check it

The census-visible count is built from payroll records, not estimates. Every quarter, every employer in California reports every employee on its payroll to the state's unemployment-insurance system — that filing is required by law. The Census Bureau's LEHD program attaches each of those job records to the employer's work location and publishes the counts by census block. So individual jobs are what is inside the number: one payroll record per job.

What no one can publish is the list itself. Federal law (Title 13 of the U.S. Code) keeps the individual records confidential — for this analysis, for SANDAG, and for the vendor that built the 2019 blend alike — and the Bureau adds statistical noise to small areas so no single employer can be identified. That is why block-level figures are approximate by design, and why the city total is the number to weigh.

Five ways to check a city's number without trusting this pipeline:

  1. OnTheMap (onthemap.ces.census.gov) — the Census Bureau's own free tool over the same data. Draw your city's boundary and read the workplace count straight from the federal source. If this page's census-visible number disagrees for the same year, this page is wrong.
  2. Your business-license file. Every city licenses its businesses. Summing the employee counts on your own license records gives an independent, bottom-up total to compare against.
  3. Data Axle Reference Solutions — free with a San Diego County library card. It lists individual employers by city with estimated headcounts; a staffer can pull the actual employer list and add it up. This is the closest legal thing to seeing each job, and the same kind of source the 2019 vendor blended.
  4. The survey cross-check on this page. The American Community Survey independently asks workers where their job is. Each city's panel above shows both federal instruments; for most cities they agree within a few hundred.
  5. The ground itself. Click any sector bar above to see the blocks where its jobs sit, with a map link for each. If what is physically on those blocks does not match, that is a findable, fixable error — say so.

The uniformed-military layer is the one part census payroll data cannot see; each city's panel above shows that derivation in full, with its sources named.
