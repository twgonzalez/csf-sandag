What we did

HCD has already issued 7th-cycle determinations to the five-year-cycle counties, and those letters disclose the full methodology — every adjustment line, formula, dataset, and national benchmark. We rebuilt that method from its public inputs (ACS, LODES, HUD point-in-time counts, DOF projections and estimates, HCD annual-progress reports, CAL FIRE damage inspections, Caltrans forecasts) and proved the rebuild against the two published determinations before applying it to San Diego County:

  • Mono County (final letter, Jan 2026): 10 of 11 adjustment lines reproduced within 0–4 units; the one remaining line (the PUMS overcrowding×cost-burden overlap) is a geography limitation of public microdata that does not apply to San Diego.
  • Humboldt County (final letter, Jul 2025): 8 of 11 adjustment lines reproduced exactly — including overcrowding, cost burden, and the overlap — and the vacancy line within 1 unit.

The full line-by-line comparison, including everything that could not be reproduced and why, is in validation_mono.md.

The logic, in one paragraph

HCD's 7th-cycle method starts from DOF's projected households at the end of the projection period, then adds what existing conditions already generate: units needed to reach healthy vacancy rates (2% owner / 7% renter), to bring overcrowding and housing cost burden down to the national rates (less an overlap credit so no household is counted twice), to replace demolitions, to house people counted in the point-in-time homeless count, and to cover disaster losses — then subtracts projected occupied units at the start of the period. For San Diego the result is dominated by the cost-burden line (136,160 units in the central scenario): 43.4% of the county's households pay 30%+ of income for housing against 31.7% nationally. Growth, the line that dominated the 6th cycle, contributes only about 54,405.

Why this is informative for preliminary SANDAG planning

  • The magnitude arrives before the letter does. Every scenario lands well above the 6th cycle's 171,685 — electeds and staff can see the order of magnitude, and which lines drive it, three years before HCD hands the region the number.
  • The band is HCD's own uncertainty, not ours. The spread (179,100–228,584) comes from choices HCD itself made differently in its two published letters (two formulas for the overcrowding/cost-burden lines), plus data vintages and the projection-period end date. Nothing in the band is an invented assumption.
  • The allocation conversation can start early. The jurisdiction table below shows where need is generated under HCD's definitions versus where the current allocation method would send it — the structural tension the 7th-cycle methodology fight will be about.

None of this substitutes for the official process: HCD consults DOF on custom projections we cannot see, and both the method and the data vintages can change by 2028.

Reading the San Diego shift

The City of San Diego stands out twice in these charts, and both shifts are properties of the measurement, not of anything that changed in the city.

Why its draft generated need (99,564) sits below its 6th-cycle allocation (108,036) even though the regional total rose by a third: the city's housing-stress rates are almost exactly the regional rates — overcrowding 6.2% in the city vs 6.7% countywide, cost burden 42.9% vs 43.4% (both far above the national 31.7% benchmark) — so under HCD's definitions the city generates need in proportion to the households it houses: 45% of the region's households, 44% of the generated need. Its 6th-cycle allocation was never proportional to households: it was driven by infrastructure.

Why the allocation method sends it 143,837 (a 44,273-unit gap): the 6th-cycle method allocates 65% of all units by transit stations, and the city holds 100 of the region's 154 rail and Rapid stations and 101 of its 140 major transit stops — 67% of the transit pot — plus 56% of the jobs component, for 63% of the total. The −44k gap is the wedge between owning two-thirds of the transit infrastructure and housing 45% of the households. The same wedge, with the opposite sign, is what the high-overcrowding, high-cost-burden jurisdictions absorb: Unincorporated County (+18,643), El Cajon (+7,037), Oceanside (+5,690), Chula Vista (+3,716), Vista (+3,584).

Because the shift comes from the allocation formula's geometry rather than from conditions inside the city, no census-tract pattern within the City of San Diego "accounts for" it. A tract-level map of the city would answer a different (and worthwhile) question — where inside the city the overcrowding and cost-burden need concentrates — and can be built from the same ACS tables at tract geography if wanted; it would not move any number in these charts.
