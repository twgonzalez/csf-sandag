The AFFH gate constant, now measured

Share of lower-income units in High / Highest Resource tracts 0.9946

Every future candidate methodology must meet or beat this number before the pipeline will emit it. The gap to 1.0000 is the statutory nonzero floor — 1% of each category spread across all tracts so all 19 jurisdictions receive units in every income category, as §65584(d)(1) requires — priced exactly.

The adopted 6th cycle has no measurable value on this axis: it allocated to jurisdictions, never to tracts, so its AFFH performance cannot be computed from the adopted documents. A tract-scored methodology is auditable on the fair-housing objective in a way the adopted one structurally cannot be.

This baseline is a measuring stick, not a proposal. It answers one question — where would units go if fair housing were the only lower-income driver — so that real candidates can be scored against it. Quoting its numbers as a recommendation misreads the design.

What the shape says: pure resource weighting moves lower-income housing out of the lower-resource jurisdictions — National City −72%, Escondido −48%, Lemon Grove −42% — and into the high-resource ones. That is the fair-housing objective as arithmetic. San Diego falls because the adopted transit component weighted it even harder than its 56% share of high-resource housing; the unincorporated county rises most in absolute units because it holds the region's largest stock. Santee, which wants more housing, gets +123% on resource grounds alone — before any capacity factor is applied.
