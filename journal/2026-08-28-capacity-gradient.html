The capacity gradient runs backwards

First measurement of physical capacity against the state's fair-housing map, on the land and hazard axes: the region's lower-resource urban core is its least constrained ground, and its high-resource areas carry the fire and protected-land burden.

What it means

Capacity-as-reducer is empirically an AFFH regression in this region — not just a legal risk.

A methodology that lowered jurisdictions' allocations for physical constraint would move units from high-resource to low-resource areas: the precise opposite of Gov. Code §65584(d)(5). Gate A asked whether the orthogonal design — capacity siting units within resource bins, never choosing the bins — is a preference or a requirement. On the axes measured: a requirement. The finding is frozen in a test, so a data revision that flips it fails the build loudly.

And the orthogonal design has real room to work. 71,267 housing units sit in tracts that are both Highest Resource and Highest Capacity; the Highest Resource row spreads across all four capacity bins. Capacity-based siting inside the AFFH gate has genuine choices to make — the gate is not a straitjacket.

What this does not yet say

01

Three of seven indicators. Fire, floodway, and protected land are measured. Sea level rise awaits the scenario decision; sewer and water await their sources; evacuation is Phase 4.

02

The two missing heavyweights are not alike. Sea level rise is near-certain to strengthen the gradient — the modelled inundation sits under the expensive coast. Evacuation is genuinely open in either direction: it is measured as bottleneck headroom on the road network, not a hazard footprint, and dense urban tracts routing through saturated interchanges could score poorly merely for being dense — which would put evacuation capacity with opportunity, against this gradient. Phase 4 measures it; nothing here assumes the answer.

03
