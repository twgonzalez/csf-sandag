Evacuation, measured

The fourth capacity dimension — the one every hard question in this project kept returning to — computed from one county road network, with every bottleneck's load attributed among the neighborhoods that share it.

What evacuation does to an allocation, in plain English

Every neighborhood now has a measured number: how many homes it can empty through the worst chokepoint on its way out — its discharge rate, in dwelling units per hour. A tract that takes nine hours to clear discharges one-ninth of its homes each hour. In a capacity-weighted methodology, that number tilts where units land. Three rules cover it:

It increases a share when the roads can absorb more

Within the group of neighborhoods already chosen on fair-housing grounds, the ones that drain well take a larger slice. A city whose good-school neighborhoods sit on a street grid with several ways out — gains, because its tracts win those comparisons. National City's grid posts the county's best discharge: 398 units/hr.

It decreases a share where everyone shares one way out

A neighborhood behind a funnel — one bridge, one highway out — takes a smaller slice, and the difference flows to better-drained neighborhoods of the same fair-housing standing. Imperial Beach discharges 100 units/hr through the Palm Avenue funnel, second-worst in the county; Coronado's bridge now shows in its number.

Three things it can never do

It never changes the regional total — 171,685 units get placed, always. It never decides which kind of neighborhood gets lower-income homes — the fair-housing gate fixes that first. And it never zeroes out a city — every jurisdiction keeps a nonzero allocation in every income category, by statute (§65584(d)(1)).

Worked example

Say 1,000 lower-income units are bound for Highest Resource neighborhoods — the gate decided that, and evacuation cannot undo it. Two such neighborhoods compete: a coastal tract discharging 350 units/hr onto I-5, and a peninsula tract behind a bridge discharging 90 units/hr. A capacity-weighted rule sends more of the 1,000 to the first and fewer to the second. If the two sit in different cities, the first city's total rises and the second's falls — as an output of where the roads are, not as a judgment about either city.

Which is also why the number is actionable: build a second way out, and the tract's discharge rate — and its share — moves. No other kind of allocation factor gives a city something to do.

Read before quoting

v1, uncalibrated, and it says so. Link capacities are federal defaults (FHWA HPMS App. N) not yet calibrated against Caltrans count stations; everyone departs at once, so clearance hours are upper bounds; each tract's traffic takes one shortest path, so street grids read worse than reality — a bias that runs against the urban cores, the direction that matters for fair housing. The measure is congestion-based, not distance-based: an hour on an empty desert road scores well. Five military-base tracts (Camp Pendleton, MCAS Miramar among them) have restricted road networks absent from the public map and snap to the freeway directly — flagged per tract. The scored form is a recorded amendment to the spec (§3.1): pro-rata discharge rate now, the headroom form and the contribution cap return with staged departures in v2.

Every number traces to reports/evacuation/evacuation_by_tract.csv and the bottleneck ledger — every loaded link, its capacity, its load, and the neighborhoods that share it. A traffic engineer can audit it link by link.
