Prepared to support SANDAG's 7th-cycle methodology discussions · 2026-08-30

Preparing for the Seventh Cycle

What an independent, fully open rebuild of the regional housing allocation found — the assumptions it made, the conclusions it supports, and the decisions the next methodology will have to make either way.

About this document

What it is. Findings from a working, independently built allocation pipeline that uses only public data. Every number here is reproducible: the code, inputs, and reports are being published in a public repository, and any figure can be recomputed from them.

What the numbers are. All allocations divide the 6th-cycle total of 171,685 homes, so alternatives can be compared against the adopted plan everyone knows. This is a test kitchen, not a draft allocation — the 7th-cycle total arrives from the state, likely 2027, and everything scales with it. The measurements underneath are current: the 2026 state opportunity map, the 2020 census, the 2024–25 fire hazard maps, today's road network.

How cities are shown. By archetype, not by name — six representative profiles with rounded, illustrative figures drawn from real runs. The point is the pattern, not any city's specific number.

Part I

The narrative

Every eight years the state assigns the region a housing number; SANDAG divides it among 19 jurisdictions. The total never changes — only the split. Fair-housing law constrains the split in one hard way: homes for lower-income families must be steered toward neighborhoods with good schools, jobs, and services, not concentrated where poverty already is. The question this project tested: can a lawful split also respect physical reality — fire, flood, protected land, evacuation — using only data anyone can check?

01

The adopted 6th cycle checks out — and cannot be checked

We rebuilt the adopted 2020 allocation from its published documents and matched all 76 line items to within two homes. The adopted arithmetic is internally sound. But matching it required reverse-engineering a reconciliation step that appears in no adopted document — a step worth up to 990 homes for a single jurisdiction. A city in its 45-day appeal window could not have recomputed its own number. This is not a criticism of the team that produced the 6th cycle under 2019's tools and timelines; it is a statement about what any team can defend without published code — and it is the institution's largest exposure going into the 7th.

02

The only correction ever made came through the most expensive channel available

The one change between draft and adopted allocations was a jobs-data fix: naval installation jobs credited to the wrong jurisdictions, worth 135 homes. It surfaced because one city retained counsel and someone called the Navy — four months after the draft issued. The lesson is not that an error occurred; errors occur in every dataset. It is that the error-correction mechanism was an appeal. An open pipeline gives staff a cheaper one: our rebuild is designed to catch that class of error automatically before a draft issues, and its acceptance test is finding that same Navy error with no hints.

03

The 2027 review will not be the 2019 review

HCD approved the 6th-cycle methodology weeks after the fair-housing objective entered the statute, with no implementing guidance in existence; the guidance arrived in 2021, and the Bay Area's methodology was pushed toward opportunity-based factors months later. The state now measures where the affordable homes land. A station-count-and-jobs methodology with an income-mix adjustment — reasonable under 2019's understanding — would face questions in 2027 it never faced then.

04

The region's best neighborhoods sit on its most constrained land — measured

Crossing the state's opportunity map with fire, flood, protected land, and evacuation capacity: the lowest-scoring neighborhoods are 3.3 times more likely to be on the least constrained land than the highest-scoring ones. The consequence is decisive for methodology design: reducing a city's total because of physical constraint shifts homes toward the region's struggling neighborhoods — the precise pattern fair-housing law prohibits. Constraint can lawfully decide where within a community homes go; it cannot decide how many a community gets.

05

An evacuation measure with no drawn boundaries — validated against real fires

We grouped neighborhoods by the escape routes they share — "evacuation sheds," implied by the road network the way watersheds are implied by terrain, with no zone drawn by any analyst — and scored each by how many households per hour its worst shared chokepoint can discharge. Tested by replaying every 15,000+ acre fire since 2000 as an evacuation: the 2003 Cedar Fire replay's binding chokepoint is Wildcat Canyon Road, where most of that fire's victims died. The model had no knowledge of the fire.

06

Safety-aware allocation works — as placement, at zero fair-housing cost

A split that weights neighborhoods by physical capacity moves roughly 7,300 homes off fire-country land onto safe urban street grids while scoring identically on the fair-housing test to four decimal places — and the software refuses to emit any split that scores worse. The refusal is enforcement, not policy: the formula cannot be aimed at or for any city.

Part II

The evidence

Design rules, stated once

  • Zero-sum, always. The regional total is an input; every run reconciles to it exactly, by income level.
  • Computed at neighborhood level. City totals are outputs of neighborhood placement, never scored directly.
  • Constraint places, never reduces. Physical capacity chooses among neighborhoods of equal fair-housing standing.
  • Prohibited factors are refused by the software, with the statute cited: permit caps, past underproduction, "stable population," anything derived from local zoning.
  • The fair-housing floor is enforced in code. No emitted split may place a smaller share of lower-income homes in high-scoring neighborhoods than the fair-housing-only benchmark (99.5%).
  • Same inputs, same outputs, byte for byte. Every data file is pinned and checksummed; every factor cites a public source.

Read the movements, not the digits. The fair-housing benchmark redraws the map — coastal and high-scoring communities rise, the urban core's total falls while its burden shifts toward market-rate homes, the metropolis loses its 2020 transit-count premium. The safety-aware column then re-sorts within that map: fire-heavy archetypes give back a fifth to a half of their benchmark increase; safe grids and low-hazard coast absorb it. Fair housing decides how many; safety decides where.

The evacuation evidence, with its limits attached

451 sheds across 737 neighborhoods; every bottleneck's load, capacity, and member neighborhoods published. Validation: household-weighted, the shed predictions matched the fire-replay chokepoints 63% of the time — strongest on the largest fires. We recorded predictions before computing: the peninsula city fell as predicted (less than predicted — its naval commuters aren't counted yet); the urban grids held as predicted; one small coastal city we expected to hold instead measured second-most constrained in the county — its single funnel road outweighed our prior. The miss stays on the record beside the hits.

Version-one limits, volunteered: road capacities are federal defaults not yet calibrated to local traffic counts; everyone departs at once (real staged departures are gentler; real gridlock physics — stalled cars, blocked intersections — are harsher, and the two do not cancel); each neighborhood's traffic takes one best route, so dense grids read worse than reality; a third of road segments carry a real lane count from the map data, the rest use class defaults; military bases' internal roads are absent from public maps and are handled explicitly. Rankings are the meaningful output. Clearance clocks are not predictions.

A structure worth studying: more affordable at the coast, more market-rate inland

One lawful design lowers high-scoring cities' headline totals while honoring fair housing: let the opportunity map place the affordable homes (pulling them coastward and toward good schools), and let employment place the market-rate homes (pulling them toward job centers). Distribution of load improves; the fair-housing test is met on the metric the state now uses.

Its three catches, stated before anyone falls in love: the affordable homes are the hard part — rezoning, density, subsidy — so a smaller total that is mostly affordable is a heavier lift than a larger total that is mostly market-rate, and an archetype reading past its headline will see roughly double its former affordable obligation. It requires job counts trustworthy enough to allocate homes with — the 6th cycle's were the closed dataset containing the Navy error, and the open replacement is the single most important unbuilt piece. And it should be tested with state housing staff informally before it appears in any public draft.

What we refused to do

  • No outcome-tuning. Asked whether the model could be adjusted until specific cities' numbers fell, we declined and recorded why: a formula tuned to a result is the closed-model problem rebuilt with extra steps.
  • Permit and production history never touches the allocation — quarantined at ingestion, per statute.
  • No factor without a public citation. A factor that cannot name its source is refused at load.
  • No prediction after the fact. Where we forecast results, the forecasts were committed before computation, and the misses published.
Part III

The decision agenda

Whatever factors the board ultimately weighs, these choices must be made — better deliberately than by default.

  1. The transit factor. Rebuild the 2019 station count from open transit feeds (comparable with the 6th cycle, but preserves a crude measure), or replace it with jobs-reachable-by-transit (a better measure, not comparable). This choice moves more homes than any other single decision.
  2. The jobs factor. Commission the open, corrected jobs count now — it gates the affordable-coastal / market-rate-inland structure and narrows every city's range.
  3. How much weight safety gets. The lawful dial runs from zero (the fair-housing benchmark) to full capacity weighting; both ends are computed here. A public board conversation about that dial is the methodology working as intended.
  4. Six income levels. Next cycle requires splitting "very low" further (acutely and extremely low). Placeholder targets should be set so trial runs can begin before the state's determination arrives.
  5. Sea-level-rise scenario and wildfire mitigation reporting — each a visible policy choice; recommend publishing raw and mitigation-adjusted exposure side by side.
  6. The informal HCD conversation. Test the safety-aware structure and the composition idea with state staff ahead of formal submission. The statutory clock — draft methodology to the state in late 2027, 60-day review, possible 45-day revision, adoption 2028 — leaves room for exactly one careless iteration. Spend it on purpose.

The recommendation that survives any factor choice: publish the code, pin the data, cite every factor, and hold the next methodology to the test the last one failed — a city should be able to recompute its own number in an afternoon, for free. Every finding in this document exists because that standard is achievable now. The proof is the repository itself.
