The Jobs Count, Then and Now

Jobs carried more than a third of the weight in the 2020 housing allocation, and the one error ever found in that plan was a jobs error. This report compares the 2019 jobs data with an open rebuild — what each counted, where they agree, and where and why they differ. City names appear here because both counts are public tables; the housing allocations themselves remain anonymized elsewhere.

An independent analysis, built entirely on public data so every claim can be checked. It offers no recommendations — it exists to raise better questions before the seventh cycle's decisions arrive.

What 2019 used — and why nobody can rebuild it today

The 6th cycle's jobs input was SANDAG's Employment Estimates: state job-space counts filled with a five-year average of census job data, supplemented by military counts from the San Diego Military Advisory Council and the Defense Manpower Data Center (Navy ship crews counted at their homeport). The blend was proprietary; only the 19 city totals were published. One input has since disappeared entirely: DMDC no longer publishes per-installation personnel data, so the 2019 jobs input could not be reconstructed today by anyone — including SANDAG.

The one error, as a reminder of the stakes

Between draft and adoption, exactly one input changed: Navy jobs. Two satellite sites — the Silver Strand Training Complex (Coronado) and the Outlying Landing Field (Imperial Beach), which hold at most 99 actual jobs — had been carrying Naval Base San Diego's numbers, and North Island's jobs were re-split 80.5/19.5 between Coronado and San Diego. It moved 135 homes. It surfaced four months after the draft, through a phone call to Naval Facilities Engineering Command, and only because one city could afford to appeal.

The open rebuild

The replacement count uses census workplace data (LODES 2023) plus two corrections, every source pinned and checksummed: a uniformed-military layer (census data cannot see a sailor — roughly 111,568 active-duty jobs, restored from published county totals, workers-by-workplace data, and military quarters counted at homeport), and a headquarters detector that catches jobs piled onto an employer's reporting address (two school districts redistributed; a single downtown tract holding 39% of the county's public-administration jobs flagged for the next roster). Tested against the one known answer: from open data alone, the two satellite sites carry no workforce, and Imperial Beach's share lands nearer the corrected 2020 numbers than the erroneous draft. The 2019 error class cannot recur by construction.

The result worth pausing on: two unrelated methods, built seven years apart — one proprietary, one fully open — independently reproduce the same employment geography for fifteen of nineteen cities. Chula Vista differs by 41 jobs. That is strong evidence both that the 2019 team's blend was sound and that an open count can stand in for it.

The four gaps, each with a name

Coronado (+0.65 pp). Two documented causes: the Navy grew (about 15% more Navy active-duty in the county between the fiscal-2019 and fiscal-2023 counts), and a convention difference — the open count assigns all of North Island to the city that contains it, where the adopted plan split the base 80.5/19.5 with San Diego by land area. Whether a base's jobs belong to the city containing its gate or are split by acreage is a definitional choice the 7th cycle should make explicitly; it is worth thousands of jobs to one small city.

National City (−0.83 pp). The naval-station convention again, in mirror image. Naval Base San Diego straddles the San Diego–National City line, and the 2019 blend — using the Navy's own site data — credited National City with roughly 14,000 of its jobs. Both open sources put those jobs in San Diego, and they agree with each other remarkably well: census workplace data counts 23,336 jobs in National City, and the ACS counts 23,034 people working there — 302 apart. As with Coronado, whether a straddling base's jobs follow the piers or the reporting address is a definitional choice the 7th cycle should make explicitly.

San Diego (−1.49 pp) and the Unincorporated County (+1.81 pp). The military layer's split between these two giants is the least certain corrected number — it inherits whatever headquarters distortion remains in the census data, and Camp Pendleton's workforce is difficult to bound with public sources. The county total and the smaller cities do not depend on it.

Why this matters for 2028

Whatever formula the 7th cycle adopts will almost certainly weigh employment, and the corrected geography says something the 2019 data also implied but nobody computed: the region's coastal cities hold more jobs per existing home than the county average (Coronado 2.8× its housing weight; Poway 1.53; Carlsbad 1.27; even the small beach cities are balanced, not job-light). Run through the allocator, every employment weighting therefore moves housing numbers toward the coast — including the structure sometimes suggested for relieving it. Still open on the jobs factor: which measure should carry weight (total jobs, jobs reachable, or jobs-housing fit — each lawful, each pointing differently), reconciliation to state sector totals, and seasonal adjustment; today's counts are second-quarter snapshots.

Questions this raises

Should a base's jobs belong to the city that contains it, or be split by acreage — and who decides before the numbers do? Should the 7th cycle adopt a jobs input that no longer exists behind closed doors, or one any city can recompute? And which definition of "jobs" serves the allocation's purpose: where the work is, where it can be reached, or whether its wages match the housing nearby?
