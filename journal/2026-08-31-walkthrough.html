The Seventh Cycle in Ten Minutes

Every eight years, the state tells this region how many new homes to plan for — last time, 171,685. SANDAG's job is to split that number among 19 cities. We rebuilt the last split, tested it against the rules coming in 2028, and found some things the board should see. This is that story, in four parts.

Part 1 · We checked the last plan

We rebuilt the 2020 housing plan, number by number

The 2020 plan is really 76 numbers: 19 cities, each with a target at 4 income levels. Using only the plan's public documents, we redid the math for every one. Each square below is one of those 76 numbers. Green means our rebuilt number matched the adopted plan.

Every square is green: the 2020 plan's math was right. We know because we rebuilt it.

Presenter notes

Open with credit: this is a compliment to the team that built the 6th cycle. The point of rebuilding was not to find mistakes — it was to establish a trustworthy starting line before testing anything new. Everything later in this deck stands on this frame.

"Within 2 homes" is rounding, nothing more. The plan reconciles exactly at the region level.

Part 1 · We checked the last plan

But nobody outside could have checked it

Here's the catch: one step of the math appears in no published document. The only way to get those 76 matches was to work that step out backwards. For the biggest city, it moved about 990 homes. The timeline below shows what "checking" looked like in real life.

Why this matters: the 2028 numbers will be bigger and harder-fought than 2020's. A number nobody can check invites an appeal or a lawsuit; a number anyone can check settles the argument. Every number in the rest of this deck is the second kind — public data, published math, open to the state and the public alike.

Presenter notes

The hidden step, if asked: the published income percentages don't add up to 100%, and fixing that the obvious way leaves the region about 640 affordable homes short of state totals. An unwritten rebalancing spreads the correction across every city; the biggest city absorbs the most — roughly 990 homes down at the top income level, 850 up at the lower ones. Every adopted number is correct. The step just isn't written anywhere.

This is not a criticism of the 2019 team, who worked with 2019's tools and deadlines. It is the biggest procedural risk going into the 7th cycle: an appeal a city can't evaluate is an appeal waiting to become a lawsuit — and Part 2 will show the 2028 allocations shifting hard enough that appeals are likely.

The point to land before advancing: this frame is not a complaint, it's the setup. Everything from here on was produced with open data and published code — the transparency the state and the public will be able to verify for themselves. Frame nine proposes making that the official standard.

Part 2 · The rules are changing

In 2019, fair housing shaped each city's mix

Fair-housing law says homes for lower-income families should go where the schools and jobs are good. The 2020 plan handled that in two steps, in this order: first, train stations and job counts set each city's total. Only then did the fairness rule act — adjusting the income mix inside each total. Watch three cities go through both steps: a coastal city with top schools, the big downtown city, and a struggling urban-core suburb.

In 2019, fairness came second. It could change each city's recipe — but by then the totals were already set. The state approved this.

Presenter notes

Give the 2019 team full credit here. Their adopted document reviewed the state's neighborhood scorecard by name and showed that the cities with the best schools and jobs received the highest affordable percentages. The state approved without changes — weeks after the fairness requirement entered the law, before the state had written any guidance on how to meet it.

The bars are schematic, but the pattern is the real one: the best-schools beach cities had few or no stations, so they ended up with small totals and the largest affordable percentages at the same time. One line to keep handy: fair housing shaped the percentages; stations and jobs shaped the totals.

Part 2 · The rules are changing

For 2028, the state expects fair housing to shape the totals

Under the rules the state has applied since 2021, fairness moves to the front of the line: the same scorecard that used to adjust the recipe now helps decide the totals themselves. Here are the same three cities. The dashed marks show their old, 2019-style totals; the solid bars are what fairness-first produces.

One move — fairness from step two to step one — resizes every bar. The next frame shows that run on the real numbers.

Presenter notes

The measuring stick behind "good schools and jobs" is the state's official neighborhood scorecard — the CTCAC/HCD "opportunity map," updated every year. It rates every neighborhood on school quality, incomes, and environment. Before relying on it, we rebuilt its published scores and matched all 638 complete neighborhoods exactly.

The guidance arrived in 2021, after our last plan was approved. The Bay Area's most recent methodology was steered toward using this scorecard to help set city totals directly. That is the bar the 2028 plan will be reviewed against.

Part 2 · The rules are changing

We ran the same 171,685 homes under the new rules

Each row below is one type of city (real cities, names removed). The black tick is its number in the 2020 plan. The shaded box is where its number lands under every legal 2028-style formula we tested — the box's width is the full range of choices the board actually has.

Look at the top four rows: every box sits to the right of its tick. None of the legal formulas we tested lowers them. The one lawful way to shrink a headline makes the hard part — the homes for lower-income families — roughly double.

Presenter notes

The two ends of each box: one end is "the fairness scorecard alone decides," the other is "same fairness rules, plus safety data" — the full legal dial. We used the 2020 total of 171,685 on purpose, so every result can be compared to numbers people already know. The state's new total arrives around 2027 and rescales everything proportionally.

Why the big city falls: it loses the bonus the 2020 formula gave its train stations — not because the fairness rules disfavor it. Why the working-class suburb falls: the fairness rules stop steering affordable homes toward it. Its mix shifts toward market-rate even as its total drops. Figures are rounded and illustrative; the direction and rough size are the point, not the digits.

Precision on "we tested": the boxes span the formulas we have run, not every design the law allows. The one lawful structure that lowers a coastal headline — the fairness map places the lower-income homes, job centers place the market-rate ones — trades the smaller headline for roughly double the lower-income obligation, and it cannot be evaluated properly until the open jobs count exists (frame 8's notes have the detail). A formula that also weighs employment will move these boxes; that analysis is queued.

Part 3 · What safety data can do

First, what it can't do: lower a city's total

A fair question: can't fire risk bring those coastal and back-country numbers down? To answer it, we measured how much of each kind of neighborhood sits on the safest land to build — away from severe fire zones, floodways, and evacuation trouble.

The total is fixed. Cut a fire-zone city's number, and those homes reappear on the safest land — which mostly means struggling neighborhoods. That's the exact outcome fair-housing law forbids. So our software refuses to compute it, for anyone.

Presenter notes

How measured: we crossed the state's neighborhood scorecard with fire severity zones, floodways, protected land, and our evacuation measure, for every neighborhood in the county. The pattern is strong and unwelcome: safety and opportunity point in opposite directions here.

What stays legal: safety can decide where within and among cities of equal fairness standing homes go. It cannot decide how many a city gets. We were asked at one point whether the model could be tuned until certain cities' numbers fell; we declined and wrote down why. A formula aimed at an answer is the closed-model problem all over again.

Part 3 · What safety data can do

So we measured evacuation — and tested it on real fires

We grouped neighborhoods by the escape routes they share, and scored each group by its worst traffic pinch point — how many households per hour can actually get out. Then the test: replay every big fire since 2000 and see if the model finds the real trouble spots. Below is the 2003 Cedar Fire replay.

The model picked Wildcat Canyon Road — the road where most of the Cedar Fire's victims died. It was never told about the fire. This data is real enough to use.

Presenter notes

Scale: 451 escape-route groups covering all 737 neighborhoods; every pinch point's load and capacity is published. The Cedar Fire result is now a permanent automated test — if a future model change stops finding Wildcat Canyon Road, the build fails.

The miss, told straight: we predicted the small coastal cities would score fine; one came in second-worst in the county because of a single funnel road we underrated. The peninsula city dropped as predicted, but less — its naval commuters aren't counted yet.

Version-one limits to volunteer: road capacities are federal defaults, not yet tuned to local traffic counts; the model sends everyone at once; each neighborhood takes one best route. Rankings are the meaningful output — the clock times are not predictions.

Part 3 · What safety data can do

Safety data can balance the 2028 numbers

Both versions below follow the 2028 fairness rules. The center line is the fairness-scorecard-alone version. Each bar shows what changes when safety data also gets a vote: bars pointing left give back part of their increase; bars pointing right take on a little more.

Fire country gives back part of its increase; safe street grids absorb it; the fairness score doesn't move. Safety — and the law's other factors, like sewer capacity and flood risk — can balance the numbers this way, within the fairness rules.

Presenter notes

Say the boundary out loud: this is balancing within the fairness rules, not escaping them. Fire-heavy communities keep an increase — they give back roughly a fifth to a half of it. The fairness floor is enforced in code: no result may place a smaller share of lower-income homes in high-scoring neighborhoods than the fairness-only version does, and the two versions here tie to four decimal places.

The other statutory factors — sewer and water capacity, floodways, sea-level rise — plug into the same slot: they help choose where, never how many. Sea-level rise needs a board decision on which scenario to use; sewer data is specified but not yet built.

If asked about lowering coastal headline totals: there is one legal structure — let the fairness scorecard place the affordable homes and let job centers place the market-rate ones. Headlines fall, but the affordable share roughly doubles, which is the expensive part; and it needs a trustworthy open jobs count, which doesn't exist yet. Worth testing quietly with state staff before anything public.

Part 4 · What happens next

Everything you just saw can be checked — that's the point

Remember the start: the 2020 plan could only be checked by reverse-engineering it, and its one error took a lawyer four months. Here is the standard we're proposing for 2028, and already meeting ourselves:

An open plan turns appeals into arithmetic. Errors get caught before the draft goes out, not four months after.

Presenter notes

What "open" means concretely: every data file is pinned and checksummed; every factor names its public source or is refused at load; forbidden factors (permit caps, past underproduction, "stable population" arguments) are refused by the software with the law cited. Same inputs, same outputs, byte for byte.

This recommendation stands no matter which factors the board ultimately picks. It is the cheapest insurance available against the 7th cycle's biggest procedural risk.

Part 4 · What happens next

Four decisions this year, against the state's clock

The deadlines below are set by state law, not by us. The one cheap chance to iterate is the informal conversation — everything after "draft to the state" is formal and timed.

The order matters: the state conversation shapes everything else, and the jobs count unlocks the most options. Both can start now.

Presenter notes

Dependencies, if asked: the informal conversation is the only free iteration the statutory schedule allows, so it comes first. The jobs count gates the affordable-coastal / market-rate-inland structure and narrows every city's range. Calibration keeps the evacuation work honest before it carries more weight. The income placeholders cover the six levels the next cycle requires, so trial runs start before the state's new total arrives (~2027), not after.

Part 4 · What happens next

Questions worth asking us

This work is built to be questioned — a hard question becomes a computer run, and the run becomes a published answer. These four are good places to start:

  1. Which of your evacuation assumptions, if wrong, would change the answer?
  2. What happens to every number if the state's next total is 30% smaller?
  3. Walk us through where your system would have caught the Navy jobs error.
  4. What exactly would have to change in state law for fire risk to lower a city's total?

Shorter: the one-pager. Deeper: the staff paper, then the public repository — where every number in this deck can be recomputed.

Presenter notes

Close on the standard, not a summary: a city should be able to recompute its own number in an afternoon, for free. Every number in this deck already meets that standard. If a question stumps us, that's the system working — we'll run it and publish the answer.
