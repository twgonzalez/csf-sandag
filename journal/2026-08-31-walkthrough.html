The Seventh Cycle in Ten Questions

Every eight years the state hands this region a housing number — last time, 171,685 homes. SANDAG splits it among 19 cities. The total never changes; only the split does. We rebuilt the split so anyone can check it, using only public data. Ten questions, ten pictures, short answers.

Presenter notes

Ten minutes: about a minute a frame. Every number shown is precomputed by the public pipeline and traceable to a committed report — nothing on these pages calculates anything. Cities appear as six anonymized archetypes with rounded figures, the same six used in the one-pager and the staff paper, so the documents corroborate each other.

The goal of the walkthrough is better questions, not finished arguments. The closing frame hands the board four of them.

01 / 10

Why did we do this?

Cities had 45 days to appeal numbers they could not check. The one real error took a lawyer and four months to surface.

Presenter notes

Land the credit first: we rebuilt the adopted 2020 allocation from its published documents and matched all 76 line items to within two homes. The adopted arithmetic is internally sound. But the match required reconstructing a reconciliation step that appears in no adopted document — worth up to 990 homes on a single city's income-mix line. The error itself was naval-installation jobs credited to the wrong cities, worth 135 homes.

This is not a criticism of the team that produced the 6th cycle with 2019's tools and timelines; it is a statement about what any team can defend without published code. If asked "so was the plan wrong?" — no; it reconciles exactly. The exposure is procedural, and it is the largest one going into the 7th cycle.

02 / 10

Didn't we already do fair housing in 2019?

Yes. 2019 used it to set each city's mix. The state now expects it to set each city's total.

Presenter notes

Credit the 2019 team explicitly. The 2019 methodology reviewed the state's opportunity map by name and reproduced it in the adopted document as evidence that the cities receiving the highest affordable percentages were the most resource-rich. The state accepted that approach without amendment — weeks after the fair-housing objective entered the statute, before any implementing guidance existed.

The guidance arrived in 2021, and the Bay Area's methodology was subsequently steered toward using opportunity geography to help set allocations directly. That is the change in review standards this frame is about — the ground moved; nobody in 2019 did it wrong.

One term to define once: the "opportunity map" is the state's official neighborhood index of school, income, and environmental conditions (the CTCAC/HCD Opportunity Map, updated annually). We replicated its published scores exactly before relying on it.

03 / 10

Why do coastal numbers go up?

Coastal cities have the schools and jobs the law points to. Every lawful formula we ran raises them.

Presenter notes

The range for each archetype spans the two ends of the lawful dial: affordable homes follow the state opportunity map and nothing else (one end), and the same placement with physical safety choosing among equally-scoring neighborhoods (the other). Coastal and high-scoring communities rise at both ends; the urban core's total falls while its mix shifts toward market-rate homes; the metropolis loses the premium the 2020 transit count gave it.

If asked about a specific city: these are archetypes with rounded, illustrative figures from real runs — the direction and rough size matter, not the digits. The state's next regional total arrives around 2027 and rescales everything.

04 / 10

Can fire risk lower a city's number?

No. The total is fixed, so homes cut from fire country reappear in struggling neighborhoods — the outcome the law forbids.

Presenter notes

The engine is zero-sum: the regional total never changes, only the split, so a reduction anywhere is an increase somewhere else. We crossed the state's opportunity map with fire, flood, protected land, and evacuation capacity: the lowest-scoring neighborhoods are 3.3 times as likely to sit on the least-constrained land as the highest-scoring ones. Reduce a fire-exposed city's total and the homes flow, mechanically, toward the region's struggling neighborhoods — the precise concentration fair-housing law prohibits.

So constraint can lawfully decide where within a community homes go; it cannot decide how many a community gets. Our software refuses to compute hazard-based reductions — for anyone, including us. The maps on the frame are stylized; the measured overlap is the 3.3× figure, reproducible from the repository.

05 / 10

Then what is safety data for?

Placing homes, not counting them. It moved 7,300 homes to safer ground. The fairness score did not budge.

Presenter notes

The 7,300 figure compares two runs: affordable homes follow the opportunity map and nothing else, versus the same placement with physical capacity choosing among equally-scoring neighborhoods. Both score 99.5% on the fair-housing test — equal to four decimal places — because the floor is a hard gate in the software: no emitted split may place a smaller share of lower-income homes in high-scoring neighborhoods than the fair-housing-only benchmark does.

If asked what safety costs the fire-heavy communities: they give back a fifth to a half of the increase the fair-housing benchmark hands them; safer areas absorb it. The fair-housing rule sets each city's level; safety sets placement within and among cities of equal standing.

06 / 10

How do we know the evacuation model is any good?

We replayed the 2003 Cedar Fire. The model flagged Wildcat Canyon Road — where most of its victims died — knowing nothing about the fire.

Presenter notes

Method in one breath: we grouped neighborhoods by the escape routes they share — derived from the road network itself, not from boundaries anyone drew — and scored each group by how many households per hour its worst shared chokepoint can discharge. 451 groups across 737 neighborhoods; every chokepoint's load, capacity, and member neighborhoods are published.

The validation replays every 15,000+ acre fire since 2000 as an evacuation and asks whether the model's chokepoints match the replay's. The Cedar Fire result is now a permanent automated test — if a future change to the model stops finding Wildcat Canyon Road, the build fails.

07 / 10

What did it get wrong?

We called three results in advance and got two. The miss is published next to the hits.

Presenter notes

The peninsula city fell less than predicted because its naval commuters are not counted yet; "second-worst" means second-most constrained of the county's cities on the discharge measure.

Volunteer the version-one limits before anyone asks: road capacities are federal defaults not yet calibrated to local traffic counts; the model departs everyone at once (real staged departures are gentler, real gridlock is harsher, and the two do not cancel); each neighborhood's traffic takes one best route, so dense grids read somewhat worse than reality. Rankings are the meaningful output; clearance clocks are not predictions.

The discipline is the point of this frame: forecasts are committed before computation and the misses are published. Calibration against real traffic counts and summer visitor loads is the next scheduled step, run the same way — predictions first.

08 / 10

What about smaller totals with more affordable homes?

Legal, and we can build it. The headline shrinks — but the hard part, the affordable homes, roughly doubles.

Presenter notes

The structure: let the opportunity map place the affordable homes (pulling them coastward, toward good schools) and let employment place the market-rate homes (pulling them toward job centers). High-scoring cities' headline totals fall, and the fair-housing test is met on the metric the state now uses. It is the modernized version of the 2019 approach.

Three catches, in order: a smaller total that is mostly affordable is a heavier lift than a larger one that is mostly market-rate — a city reading past its headline sees roughly double its former affordable obligation. It needs job counts trustworthy enough to allocate homes with, and the 6th cycle's jobs data was the closed dataset that contained the Navy error — the open replacement is the most important unbuilt piece. And it should be tested with state housing staff informally before it appears in any public draft.

09 / 10

What has to be decided, and when?

Four decisions this year. The state's clock does the rest.

Presenter notes

The schedule is statutory, not chosen: draft methodology to the state in late 2027, a 60-day state review, a possible 45-day revision, adoption in 2028. That is why the informal state conversation comes first — it is the only cheap iteration available.

The order encodes dependencies: the state conversation shapes which structures are worth building out; the jobs count gates the composition option and narrows every city's range (its acceptance test is finding the Navy error unaided); calibration keeps the evacuation work honest before it carries more weight — predictions logged first, misses published; the income-level placeholders cover the six income levels the next cycle requires, so trial runs start before the state's total arrives (~2027) rather than after.

10 / 10

Questions worth asking us

We are after better questions, not finished arguments. Start with these four.

  1. Which of your evacuation assumptions, if wrong, would change the answer?
  2. What happens to every number if the state's next total is 30% smaller?
  3. Walk us through where your system would have caught the Navy jobs error.
  4. What exactly would have to change in state law for fire risk to lower a city's total?

Shorter: the one-pager. Deeper: the staff paper, then the repository — every number here can be recomputed.

Presenter notes

Close on the invitation, not a summary. If a question stumps us, that is the system working: everything here is recomputable in public, so a hard question becomes a run, and the run becomes a published answer. The standard we are asking the next methodology to meet is the one the last one could not: a city should be able to recompute its own number in an afternoon, for free.
