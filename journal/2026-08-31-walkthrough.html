The Seventh Cycle in Ten Minutes

Every eight years the state tells this region how many new homes to plan for — last time, 171,685 — and SANDAG splits the number among 19 cities. The rules for the next split, due in 2028, have changed. This deck is the board's early look: what the new rules do to each city's number, which levers remain and what each one actually does, and which decisions are still open this year. Ten minutes now, so nothing in 2028 arrives as a surprise — and every figure can be recomputed from public data.

Part 1 · We checked the last plan

But nobody outside could have checked it

Here's the catch: one step of the math appears in no published document. The only way to get those 76 matches was to work that step out backwards. For the biggest city, it moved about 990 homes. The timeline below shows what "checking" looked like in real life.

Why this matters: the 2028 numbers will be bigger and harder-fought than 2020's. A number nobody can check invites an appeal or a lawsuit; a number anyone can check settles the argument. Every number in the rest of this deck is the second kind — public data, published math, open to the state and the public alike.

SOURCES · Data: 6th Cycle Methodology (2019), Tables 2–4 · Final Plan (2020), p. 14 (the correction) · data/reference/sixth_cycle_jobs.csv — Method: reports/sixth_cycle_replication.md · docs/status.md — Code: allocate/sixth_cycle.py

Presenter notes

The hidden step, if asked: the published income percentages don't add up to 100%, and fixing that the obvious way leaves the region about 640 affordable homes short of state totals. An unwritten rebalancing spreads the correction across every city; the biggest city absorbs the most — roughly 990 homes down at the top income level, 850 up at the lower ones. Every adopted number is correct. The step just isn't written anywhere.

This is not a criticism of the 2019 team, who worked with 2019's tools and deadlines. It is the biggest procedural risk going into the 7th cycle: an appeal a city can't evaluate is an appeal waiting to become a lawsuit — and Part 2 will show the 2028 allocations shifting hard enough that appeals are likely.

The point to land before advancing: this frame is not a complaint, it's the setup. Everything from here on was produced with open data and published code — the transparency the state and the public will be able to verify for themselves. Frame nine proposes making that the official standard.

Part 2 · The rules are changing

In 2019, fair housing shaped each city's mix

Fair-housing law says homes for lower-income families should go where the schools and jobs are good. The 2020 plan handled that in two steps, in this order: first, train stations and job counts set each city's total. Only then did the fairness rule act — adjusting the income mix inside each total. Watch three cities go through both steps: a coastal city with top schools, the big downtown city, and a struggling urban-core suburb.

In 2019, fairness came second. It could change each city's recipe — but by then the totals were already set. The state approved this.

SOURCES · Data: 6th Cycle Methodology (2019), pp. 6–12, income-mix adjustment and opportunity-map review — Method: docs/affh_in_the_6th_cycle.md — Code: allocate/equity.py

Presenter notes

Give the 2019 team full credit here. Their adopted document reviewed the state's neighborhood scorecard by name and showed that the cities with the best schools and jobs received the highest affordable percentages. The state approved without changes — weeks after the fairness requirement entered the law, before the state had written any guidance on how to meet it.

The bars are schematic, but the pattern is the real one: the best-schools beach cities had few or no stations, so they ended up with small totals and the largest affordable percentages at the same time. One line to keep handy: fair housing shaped the percentages; stations and jobs shaped the totals.

Part 2 · The rules are changing

For 2028, the state expects fair housing to shape the totals

Under the rules the state has applied since 2021, fairness moves to the front of the line: the same scorecard that used to adjust the recipe now helps decide the totals themselves. Here are the same three cities. The dashed marks show their old, 2019-style totals; the solid bars are what fairness-first produces.

One move — fairness from step two to step one — resizes every bar. The next frame shows that run on the real numbers.

SOURCES · Data: CTCAC/HCD Opportunity Map, 2026 edition (replicated 638/638) · Gov. Code §65584(d)(5) · HCD AFFH guidance (2021) — Method: reports/opportunity_map.md — Code: metrics/opportunity.py

Presenter notes

The measuring stick behind "good schools and jobs" is the state's official neighborhood scorecard — the CTCAC/HCD "opportunity map," updated every year. It rates every neighborhood on school quality, incomes, and environment. Before relying on it, we rebuilt its published scores and matched all 638 complete neighborhoods exactly.

The guidance arrived in 2021, after our last plan was approved. The Bay Area's most recent methodology was steered toward using this scorecard to help set city totals directly. That is the bar the 2028 plan will be reviewed against.

Part 2 · The rules are changing

We ran the same 171,685 homes under the new rules

Each row below is one type of city (real cities, names removed). The black tick is its number in the 2020 plan. The shaded box is where its number lands under every legal 2028-style formula we tested — the box's width is the full range of choices the board actually has.

Look at the top four rows: every box sits to the right of its tick. None of the five formulas tested lowers them. The employment-weighted ones are the next frame.

SOURCES · Data: Opportunity Map 2026 · 2020 Census P.L. 94-171 units · corrected LODES 2023 jobs · capacity layers — Method: reports/delta_vs_sixth_cycle.md · reports/jobs_runs.md — Code: allocate/model.py · params/ (all five formulas)

Presenter notes

Each box spans five runs: the fairness scorecard alone; fairness plus safety data; jobs-weighted siting; the "composition" structure (fairness places lower-income homes, job locations place the rest); and composition plus safety. Adding employment — the factor most certain to appear in any real 2028 formula — widened the boxes upward for the coast and pulled the big city part-way back toward its 2020 figure. We used the 2020 total of 171,685 on purpose, so every result compares to numbers people already know; the state's new total arrives around 2027 and rescales everything proportionally.

Why the big city falls: it loses the bonus the 2020 formula gave its train stations — not because the fairness rules disfavor it. Why the working-class suburb falls: the fairness rules stop steering affordable homes toward it. Its mix shifts toward market-rate even as its total drops. Figures are rounded and illustrative; the direction and rough size are the point, not the digits.

Precision on "tested": five formulas is still not every design the law allows, but the set covers the factor families any real 2028 methodology would draw from — fairness, safety, employment, and their combinations. The employment results are the next frame.

The jobs data points the same direction

Any realistic 2028 formula will also weigh employment, so we added the region's job locations — including a structure sometimes suggested for reducing coastal totals, where the fairness map places the homes for lower-income families and job locations place the rest. SOURCES · Data: LODES8 2023 with corrections (SDMAC MEIR 2024 Exh. 6 · Census 2020 P.L. 94-171 · ACS B08604 · CDE School Directory) — Method: reports/jobs_adjustments.md · reports/jobs_runs.md · predictions: docs/predictions_jobs_runs.md — Code: metrics/adjustments/ · params/composition_jobs.toml

Presenter notes

Why it backfires, in one breath: sending market-rate homes to "where the jobs are" sends them to the coast, because our coastal cities hold more jobs per existing home than the county average — the small high-resource beach cities sit at 1.05–1.27 times their housing weight, and the military-heavy coastal city at 2.8 (its base jobs are now correctly counted). Only one mid-size coastal city is genuinely job-light, and it is the only one this structure lowers.

For the record: the expected results were written down before the runs and scored after (docs/predictions_jobs_runs.md); the full tables are in reports/jobs_runs.md.

If a board member raises the economist's objection — "coastal land is close to fixed; supply there barely responds; these numbers cannot physically be absorbed" — give the honest boundary: these are planning obligations (what must be zoned for), not construction forecasts, and state law expressly excludes build-out and stable-population arguments from allocation factors. The absorption question is real economics, but its proper address is the state's regional total and the state's rules, not the regional split. What the split CAN do about physical reality is exactly Part 3: place the obligation on the safest, most absorbable ground — which is why the safety data matters more, not less, when every formula points coastward.

Part 3 · What the data can do

Measuring safety: fire, flood, and the way out

Balancing on safety starts with measuring it. Fire and flood zones come from published maps; the missing piece was evacuation. We measured it the way nature defines a watershed: the same neighborhoods the state's fairness map rates were grouped into evacuation sheds — everyone whose escape routes drain to one shared pinch point — drawn by the road network itself, blind to city lines. Each shed is scored by how many households per hour its pinch point can pass, and we tested the scores by replaying every big fire since 2000. Below: the 2003 Cedar Fire replay.

The replay finds Wildcat Canyon Road — where most of that fire's victims died — knowing nothing about the fire. Every neighborhood's safety is now a number the formula can use. The next two frames: what it may not do with that number, and what it does.

SOURCES · Data: OpenStreetMap road network · ACS B25044 vehicles per household · CAL FIRE FRAP fire perimeters (≥15,000 acres, 2000–) — Method: reports/evacuation_sheds.md · reports/evacuation_capacity.md — Code: metrics/sheds.py · metrics/evacuation.py

Presenter notes

If asked who drew the shed boundaries: nobody. They fall out of the road network the way watersheds fall out of terrain — change the roads and the sheds change; no analyst and no city line is involved. And because sheds are built from the same census tracts the fairness map rates, safety and fairness are measured on identical units — which is what lets the formula choose safer ground among neighborhoods of equal fairness standing, two frames ahead.

Scale: 451 escape-route groups covering all 737 neighborhoods; every pinch point's load and capacity is published. The Cedar Fire result is now a permanent automated test — if a future model change stops finding Wildcat Canyon Road, the build fails.

The miss, told straight: we predicted the small coastal cities would score fine; one came in second-worst in the county because of a single funnel road we underrated. The peninsula city dropped as predicted, but less — its naval commuters aren't counted yet.

Version-one limits to volunteer: road capacities are federal defaults, not yet tuned to local traffic counts; the model sends everyone at once; each neighborhood takes one best route. Rankings are the meaningful output — the clock times are not predictions.

The larger point to land: the law fixes how many homes; the region still decides where they go — and where determines the load on roads like this one when the fire comes. Placement is the life-safety decision the region still owns, and it can now be made with measurement.

Part 3 · What the data can do

What safety may not do: cut a city for being risky

Follow the homes. The regional total never changes, so a cut in one city must reappear in other cities — and homes pushed off hazardous land go where the safest land is. We measured where that is, using the fire, flood, and evacuation scores from the last frame.

A fire cut doesn't remove homes — it moves them, disproportionately into struggling neighborhoods, which is the concentration fair-housing law forbids. So the formula may never aim safety at a city. What it may aim at instead is the next frame.

SOURCES · Data: CAL FIRE FHSZ (SRA 2024 / LRA 2025) · FEMA NFHL floodway · CPAD 2026a · evacuation sheds · Opportunity Map 2026 — Method: reports/capacity_map.md — Code: metrics/capacity.py

Presenter notes

How measured: we crossed the state's neighborhood scorecard with fire severity zones, floodways, protected land, and our evacuation measure, for every neighborhood in the county. Adding evacuation made the pattern stronger, not weaker — the overlap was 2.7× on land and hazard alone, 3.3× once evacuation capacity entered. Safety and opportunity point in opposite directions here.

Precision if pressed: the law does not forbid housing in struggling neighborhoods — it directs the lower-income homes toward high-scoring ones. Market-rate homes can and do go to the safe grids; that is exactly where the balancing frame's absorption lands. The collision is specifically between where evacuation capacity is best and where the lower-income homes must go.

What stays legal: safety can decide where within and among cities of equal fairness standing homes go. It cannot decide how many a city gets.

Part 3 · What the data can do

What safety does: balance the 2028 numbers

The formula never sees a city. It places homes neighborhood by neighborhood, and safety only picks among neighborhoods the fairness rules rate equally. A city's total is just the sum of its neighborhoods — so totals still shift, and that shift is what the bars show. Both versions below follow the 2028 fairness rules. The center line is the fairness-scorecard-alone version. Each bar shows what changes when safety data also gets a vote: bars pointing left give back part of their increase; bars pointing right take on a little more.

No city is cut for being risky — fire country still grows, just less, because fewer of its risky corners get chosen. The signature of the difference is the fairness score: aim at a city and it breaks; choose ground and it holds at 99.5%, identical.

SOURCES · Data: as the two prior frames — Method: reports/delta_vs_sixth_cycle.md (three-column delta) — Code: params/capacity_within_bins.toml · allocate/model.py (fairness gate: enforce_affh_gate)

Presenter notes

The clean test for a skeptical questioner, in two parts: what did the formula aim at (a neighborhood's ground, or a city's total?), and what happened to the fairness score (identical, or broken?). These runs aim only at ground and tie the score to four decimal places. Say the boundary out loud: this is balancing within the fairness rules, not escaping them. Fire-heavy communities keep an increase — they give back roughly a fifth to a half of it. Nothing shown here scores below the fairness-only version; the two versions tie on the fairness test to four decimal places.

The other statutory factors — sewer and water capacity, floodways, sea-level rise — plug into the same slot: they help choose where, never how many. Sea-level rise needs a board decision on which scenario to use; sewer data is specified but not yet built.

If asked about lowering coastal headline totals: we tested the one legal structure sometimes suggested for it — the jobs frame earlier in this part. It raises job-rich coastal totals; only a genuinely job-light coastal city falls. On current data it is not a coastal relief valve.

Part 3 · What the data can do

A studio and a four-bedroom house count the same

The state's ledger counts units — it does not record their size. Below, the combined fairness-baseline obligation of four high-scoring coastal cities (about 17,000 units) is converted to bedrooms two ways: built at the mix those neighborhoods have today, and built at the small-unit mix that tends to pencil on expensive coastal land. The ledger records both as complete.

The state sets how many units and, increasingly, where — and the rezoning that follows is written in units per acre, which favors small ones. What cities keep is narrower: bedroom standards on the subsidized share, incentives for family-sized homes, and measuring what actually gets built.

SOURCES · Data: ACS 2023 5-year, Tables B25041 (bedrooms) and B25001 (units) — Method: reports/bedroom_lens.md · predictions: docs/predictions_bedroom_lens.md — Code: report/bedroom_lens.py

Presenter notes

Conversion conventions, if asked: ACS bedroom counts, a studio counted as one sleeping room and "five or more" as five — both choices shrink the measured gap, so 32,469 is the conservative figure. The gap barely moves across the five formula runs (28,000–37,000 across the sensitivity band), because it lives in the unit-counting rule, not in any allocation choice.

Two measured results worth volunteering: the family-size premium of high-scoring neighborhoods is thinner than assumed (2.79 bedrooms per unit vs 2.62 countywide, about 7%), and two of the five high-scoring coastal cities already average county-level or smaller homes (2.61 and 2.59). The small-unit tilt at the coast is not a projection; it is underway. Both figures ran against written-down expectations and one of them surprised us — the record is in reports/bedroom_lens.md.

Be straight about the limits if pressed, because the obvious objection is right: zoning is written in units per acre, not bedrooms per acre, and the rezonings the state's numbers force default to densities that favor small units. A hard bedroom mandate on market-rate projects is weaker than it looks — density-bonus law lets builders waive standards that make a project infeasible, streamlining laws bypass local discretion, and the state reviews cost-adding requirements as constraints to remove. A mandate that stops projects delivers no bedrooms at all.

What survives: requiring the subsidized share of a project to match the market units' bedroom mix (standard inclusionary practice); incentives — extra density or lower fees for family-sized homes — which waivers do not reach; family-need factors such as overcrowding, which are on the statute's own factor list for allocation; and a bedroom ledger published beside the unit ledger, which nothing can waive. Narrow tools, but real ones — and measurement is the precondition for all the rest.

An open plan turns appeals into arithmetic. Errors get caught before the draft goes out, not four months after.

Presenter notes

What "open" means concretely: every data file is pinned and checksummed; every factor names its public source or is refused at load; forbidden factors (permit caps, past underproduction, "stable population" arguments) are refused by the software with the law cited. Same inputs, same outputs, byte for byte.

This recommendation stands no matter which factors the board ultimately picks. It is the cheapest insurance available against the 7th cycle's biggest procedural risk.

Part 4 · What happens next

Questions worth asking ourselves

Every part of this deck ends in a decision, and every decision starts as a question. These are the ones the seventh cycle puts in front of us:

  1. Every lawful formula moves the numbers coastward. Do our coastal cities hear that from us this year — or from the state's draft in 2028?
  2. The one relief idea we tested did the opposite of what everyone assumed. Which of our other beliefs are we still repeating untested?
  3. The law leaves us one dial: how much weight safety carries. Where do we set it, and can we defend that setting in public, in plain words?
  4. A studio and a four-bedroom count the same in the state's ledger. When our units are built, will they hold the families the fairness law is written for — and who measures that if we don't?
  5. Could every one of our cities recompute its own number today? If not, are we willing to adopt a methodology that fails the test the last cycle failed?

Shorter: the one-pager. Deeper: the staff paper, then the public repository — where every number in this deck can be recomputed.
