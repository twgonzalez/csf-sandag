SANDAG region · 7th-cycle housing methodology · 2026-09-01

Findings & Open Questions

Every eight years the state tells this region how many new homes to plan for — last time, 171,685 — and SANDAG splits the number among 19 cities. The rules for the next split, due in 2028, have changed. This page is the running ledger of what an open rebuild of the allocation has found so far, and what it has not settled.

An independent analysis, built entirely on public data so every claim can be checked. It offers no recommendations — it exists to raise better questions before the seventh cycle's decisions arrive.

Method, in one line: every input is pinned and checksummed; expected results were written down before each analysis ran and scored after, with misses published; one command (make all) rebuilds every number. Cities appear as anonymized archetypes with rounded figures; the repository carries the full tables.

What the analysis found

01

The 2020 plan's math was right — and effectively uncheckable

Rebuilt from its published documents, all 76 lines match within two homes (51 exactly). But the match required reverse-engineering a reconciliation step no document describes, worth up to 990 homes on one city's line — and the plan's only real error (Navy jobs in the wrong cities, 135 homes) took a formal appeal and four months to surface. Cities had 45 days to object to numbers they could not recompute.

reports/sixth_cycle_replication.md · allocate/sixth_cycle.py
02

The rules moved from the mix to the totals

The 2019 methodology addressed fair housing through each city's income mix, and the state approved. Under guidance issued since 2021, the state now expects the fairness map — or something like it — to help set how many homes each city gets.

docs/affh_in_the_6th_cycle.md · CTCAC/HCD Opportunity Map 2026 (replicated 638/638)
03

Every lawful formula tested moves the numbers coastward

Five formulas — fairness alone, fairness + safety, jobs-weighted, the composition structure, and composition + safety — all run against the same 171,685 homes. The high-scoring coastal and back-country archetypes rise under every one; the big city and the working-class suburb fall under every one.

04

The structure suggested for coastal relief raises coastal numbers instead

Letting job locations place the market-rate homes was expected to shrink coastal totals. Measured: the job-rich coastal cities rise (+11%, +4%) and only one genuinely job-light coastal city falls (−9%) — because the region's coastal cities hold more jobs per existing home than the county average. The affordable share of the small high-resource coastal archetype went down (59% → 57%), not up.

05

Safety and opportunity point in opposite directions here

The safest land to build on — away from severe fire and floodways, with the road capacity to evacuate — is 3.3× more likely to sit in a struggling neighborhood than near the best schools and jobs (52.7% vs 19.4%). Adding evacuation strengthened the pattern (2.7× → 3.3×); the county's single best evacuation performance belongs to a struggling-neighborhood street grid. Consequence: cutting a fire-zone city's total sends those homes exactly where fair-housing law says lower-income homes must not concentrate — so hazard cannot lawfully reduce any city's number.

06

Evacuation is measurable, objectively, and the measure survives testing

451 evacuation sheds — neighborhoods grouped by the escape routes they share, drawn by the road network itself, blind to city lines — cover all 737 neighborhoods. Replaying every 15,000+ acre fire since 2000, the model's pinch points matched 63% of the time; the 2003 Cedar Fire replay independently found Wildcat Canyon Road, where most of that fire's victims died. One of three advance predictions was wrong (a funnel road we underrated) and is published with the hits.

reports/evacuation_sheds.md · reports/evacuation_capacity.md
07

Safety data balances the numbers — it cannot and need not cut them

Used to choose among neighborhoods of equal fairness standing, safety moves roughly 7,300 homes onto safer ground: fire-heavy archetypes give back a fifth to a half of their increases, safe street grids absorb them, and the fairness score is identical to four decimal places (99.5%) on every run. The formula never sees a city; totals shift only as the sum of their neighborhoods.

reports/delta_vs_sixth_cycle.md · allocate/model.py (fairness gate)
08

The state's ledger counts units, and families need bedrooms

The same four-city coastal obligation delivers 49,536 bedrooms built at today's neighborhood mix or 17,067 built as studios and one-bedrooms — a 32,469-bedroom difference the unit ledger cannot record, stable across all five formulas. The coastal small-unit tilt is already underway: two of the five high-resource coastal cities average county-level or smaller homes today. Cities' remaining tools are narrow — bedroom standards on the subsidized share, incentives, and measurement — because zoning is written in units per acre and mandates are waivable.

reports/bedroom_lens.md · predictions committed first: docs/predictions_bedroom_lens.md (2 of 4 hit; misses published)
09

An open jobs count is buildable — and it changes the picture

The standard open jobs data cannot see uniformed military: roughly 111,568 active-duty jobs, restored from public sources (county totals, workers-by-workplace, military quarters at homeport). The 6th cycle's Navy error cannot recur by construction — from open data alone, the misattributed sites carry no workforce. A headquarters detector also found school-district job pile-ups (two districts redistributed) and flagged the next one: a single downtown tract carries 39% of the county's public-administration jobs. One open divergence: counting a base's jobs in the city that contains it roughly doubles the military-heavy coastal city's employment versus the adopted convention — a definitional choice the 7th cycle should make explicitly.

10

Measurement is what lets capacity count again

The state removed local discretion because capacity claims arrived as assertions no one could verify — and the ban caught the honest questions along with the pretexts: the 2020 formula contained no physical-capacity measure at all. A capacity claim anyone can recompute is evidence, not excuse. That is the footing this work proposes for the seventh cycle: transparent methods, objective measurement, capacity — safety, infrastructure, economics — answered with data.

What remains open

Questions the region should be asking itself

  1. Every lawful formula moves the numbers coastward. Do the coastal cities hear that from the region this year — or from the state's draft in 2028?
  2. The relief idea everyone assumed would work did the opposite when run. What else does the region believe about this cycle that has never actually been run?
  3. The law leaves one dial: how much weight safety carries. Where should it be set — and can that setting be defended in public, in plain words?
  4. A studio and a four-bedroom count the same in the state's ledger. When the units are built, will they hold the families the fairness law is written for — and who measures that if the region doesn't?
  5. Last cycle, no city could check its own number. Will the 2028 method be published so completely that every city can recompute its own?

Questions that belong in the state conversation

  • Does a unit-counted ledger serve the families the fairness objective is written for, when the units that pencil on high-cost land are the smallest ones?
  • Should a base's jobs belong to the city that contains it, or be split by acreage? The two conventions differ by thousands of jobs for one small city.
  • Coastal land supply barely responds to obligations. If absorption is a real constraint, its address is the regional total and the state's rules — what evidence would the state accept?

Decisions pending this year

  • The informal state conversation — the only free iteration the statutory clock allows (draft late 2027, 60-day review, possible 45-day revision, adoption 2028).
  • The transit factor — rebuild the 2019 station count from open transit feeds, or replace it with jobs-reachable-by-transit. Moves more homes than any other single choice.
  • The sea-level-rise scenario — which scenario to plan against is a policy choice, not a default.
  • Placeholder targets for the six income levels the next cycle requires, so trial runs precede the state's ~2027 total.

On the workbench (known limits, queued work)

  • Evacuation calibration against real traffic counts and seasonal loads — road capacities are federal defaults; departure is modeled all-at-once; rankings are the meaningful output, clock times are not predictions.
  • Jobs count: QCEW sector reconciliation and seasonal annualisation remain unbuilt — counts are Q2 snapshots. The big-city / unincorporated military split is the least certain corrected number.
  • Which jobs measure should carry weight: total jobs, jobs-reachable, or jobs-housing fit — each lawful, each pointing differently.
  • Headquarters rosters beyond school districts (city and county government, hospital systems), and an enrollment-based size proxy for the school correction.
  • Sewer and water capacity data (specified, unbuilt); a family-need allocation factor (overcrowding is on the statute's own factor list) — designed, not yet run.
